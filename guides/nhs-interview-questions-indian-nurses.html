NHS interview questions for Indian nurses: values, scenarios and answers

For nurses from India interviewing with NHS trusts, often over Microsoft Teams, for Band 5 posts in the UK.

Updated 5 October 2026 · 8 min read · Published by Interview Sarthi, makers of Prep Sarthi (AI mock interviews) and Live Sarthi (interview help on Windows)

Short answer: NHS trusts interview internationally educated nurses with values-based and scenario questions: why the NHS, a time you showed compassion, a deteriorating patient, safeguarding and medication errors. Learn the NHS values and the 6Cs, answer with real ward examples using STAR, and use UK terms such as NEWS2, SBAR and escalation. Never pay a recruiter for an NHS job.

Not immigration or legal advice. Visa and work-permit rules change. Check the official source for your situation (the NMC and GOV.UK for nurses moving to the UK) and your university or employer's immigration adviser.

What NHS panels are listening for

Most NHS interviews are values-based: the panel wants evidence that you will act on the values in the NHS Constitution, which are working together for patients; respect and dignity; commitment to quality of care; compassion; improving lives; and everyone counts. Nursing panels also often refer to the 6Cs: care, compassion, competence, communication, courage and commitment. Name the value in your answer only when it is natural; show it through what you did.

Questions you are likely to be asked

“Why do you want to work for the NHS?”

“I've worked for six years in a 40-bed medical ward in Kochi, and the part of my job I care about most is spotting a patient getting worse early. The NHS's structured approach to that, with NEWS2 and clear escalation, is how I want to practise, and this trust's preceptorship programme would help me adapt to UK practice quickly.”

A professional reason plus something specific about the trust. Read the trust's website and values before the interview.

“Your patient's NEWS2 score has gone up from 2 to 6. What do you do?”

“I'd assess the patient straight away using ABCDE: airway, breathing, circulation, disability, exposure. I'd stay with them, ask a colleague to get the observations machine and any equipment, and escalate to the nurse in charge and the doctor following the trust's escalation policy for that score. I'd hand over using SBAR: situation, background, assessment, recommendation. Then I'd increase the frequency of observations, document everything, and keep the patient and family informed.”

Scenario answers are scored on safety: assess, escalate, communicate, document. Follow your trust's local policy for the exact thresholds.

“Tell me about a time you made a mistake.”

Pick a real, small error, for example a missed observation or a late medication. Say what happened, that you reported it straight away (in the UK through the trust's incident reporting system), what you did to keep the patient safe, and what you changed afterwards. Panels are checking honesty and the duty of candour, not looking for a perfect record.

“You suspect a vulnerable patient is being abused at home. What do you do?”

“I'd make sure the patient is safe now, listen without leading them, write down exactly what was said and seen, and report it the same day to the safeguarding lead, following the trust's safeguarding policy. I wouldn't promise to keep it secret, and I wouldn't investigate myself.”

“A patient refuses their medication. What do you do?”

“I'd find out why, explain the reason for the medicine and the risks of not taking it, and respect the decision if they have capacity. I'd document the refusal and inform the prescriber. If I had doubts about their capacity to decide, I'd raise it with the team so it can be assessed under the Mental Capacity Act.”

Other common questions: a time you showed compassion, a disagreement with a colleague, how you prioritise six patients on a busy shift, what you would do if you saw a colleague being disrespectful to a patient (raise it; the NHS calls this speaking up), and how you keep your knowledge current.

Registration and recruiters

  • NMC registration for internationally educated nurses involves an English language test and the Test of Competence (a computer-based test and the OSCE). Check the current requirements on the NMC website.
  • Never pay to get an NHS job. The code of practice for international recruitment of health and social care staff in England says candidates should not be charged recruitment fees. A request for money for a job offer or a “guaranteed placement” is a warning sign.
  • Interviews are often on Microsoft Teams from India. Test the call the day before, sit somewhere quiet with your registration documents to hand, and confirm the time zone: UK time is 4.5 or 5.5 hours behind India depending on the season.
